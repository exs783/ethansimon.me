Mission Model

A mission-performance simulator that sweeps every motor, propeller, and battery combination it is given. The model runs a theoretical mission, the DARPA 2028 heavy lift, and returns the feasibility of each combination. It's state based, with the states of landed, take-off, transition, cruise, and land. The model runs a system of dynamics equations to model the motion of flight in every state except transition. In the transition state the drone uses a quaternion model to better represent its motion through the transition from vertical to horizontal flight.

DisciplineSimulation
Year2026
Built withPython, NumPy, SciPy
Repositoryexs783/Log_Reader

Overview

Mission Model simulates the running of a course for a variety of airframes. The current iteration runs the following mission: takeoff to 100 ft, cruise 1 nautical mile, land, pick up cargo, takeoff, cruise 8 nm, land, drop cargo, takeoff, cruise 1 nm back, land. For every candidate airframe, it sweeps every motor × propeller × battery combination it has spec data for and reports which one actually completes the mission. It also reports back how much battery it has left over, and how long it can hover.

The airframe list (Hexacopter, Quadcopter, x8, Tail Sitter) matches the real vehicle classes I work on as Mechanical and Aerospace Team Lead on the VTOL team — this is the same kind of simulation-to-inform-design work as the MATLAB/Python scripts I write for the team, built out as a standalone public tool.

Method

Every TailSitter vehicle config is sized using Wing Optimizer's energy-based (hover + cruise) objective, evaluated at each tier's real governed cruise speed. The required wing area scales as 1/v², so getting the real cruise speed right has a large impact on the sizing result.

Hover time

Hover is the baseline number in the fleet chart. Each motor holds its share of the total mass, so I solve the motor's thrust curve for the throttle that does it, then divide the battery capacity by the current the whole rotor set draws at that throttle.

Hover throttle and endurance
Tm(θh)=mtotNthover=60·CAhN·I(θh)min

Tm is one motor's thrust curve, N the rotor count, CAh the battery capacity, and I the per-motor current. I find θh on 40–100% throttle with Brent's method against the continuous curve. If a motor can't reach mtot/N even at full throttle, hover time is zero.

One equation for every takeoff and every multirotor leg

A takeoff, and every leg a wingless multirotor flies, is the same differential equation: constant thrust against quadratic drag. It has an exact tanh solution, so every accel or decel leg is solved in one evaluation instead of stepped through a simulation loop.

Thrust against quadratic drag
dvdt=a0−kv2k=ρCdA2m a0=T−Wm(takeoff)a0=T2−W2m(multirotor cruise)
Closed-form solution
v(t)=vttanh(kvtt+c0)x(t)=lncosh(kvtt+c0)−lncosh(c0)k vt=a0kc0=artanh(v0vt)

vt is the terminal velocity where thrust and drag balance. Each leg stops at whichever comes first, the target speed or the distance cap. Reaching v* takes t = [artanh(v*/vt) − c0]/(k·vt), and covering a distance d takes ud = arcosh(cosh c0 · ekd). I compute ln cosh x as |x| − ln 2 + ln(1 + e−2|x|) so an underpowered vehicle with a large k·d doesn't overflow.

Cruise speed is an output of the throttle

I don't prescribe a cruise speed. Each leg settles at the speed where drag balances the forward thrust the throttle leaves over. Tilting the rotors to push forward costs weight support, so forward thrust is what remains after supporting the weight the wing doesn't carry, and the tilt inflates the frontal area that drag sees.

Equilibrium speed under tilt and wing lift
Th=T2−(W−L)2L=12ρCLSv2 Aeff=ATTh+CD0SCdTh=12ρCdAeffveq2

I solve the right-hand equation for veq with Brent's method, and fly each leg at min(0.995·veq, vmax). The cap vmax is 18 m/s (about 35 kt): a bare thrust-drag balance comes out at triple-digit speeds for these small frontal areas, which no cargo VTOL in this weight class will fly.

Braking uses the rotors

The decel leg is the accel equation with the thrust pointed the other way, so it has a closed form too. I first let the vehicle coast down on drag alone, but a coast takes time proportional to 1/k, so a lower-drag vehicle took longer to finish a leg. That's backwards for a VTOL that can brake with its rotors.

Powered braking down to the approach speed
dvdt=−(a0+kv2)b=a0k x=12klna0+kv02a0+kvf2t=arctan(v0/b)−arctan(vf/b)kb

v0 is the cruise speed the leg starts from, and vf is the 1 m/s landing-approach floor.

Energy and feasibility

Battery use is current times time, with the current read off the same throttle curve as the thrust. A configuration is feasible if the legs together stay inside the battery's rated capacity. Energy adds across legs and no leg's time budget constrains another, so maximizing leftover energy is the same as minimizing each of the six powered legs (three takeoffs, three cruises) on its own. That turns one joint throttle search into six one-dimensional sweeps over 40–100% throttle.

Per-leg energy and the feasibility test
Eleg=N·I(θ)·tleg3600∑legsEleg≤Crated

E in mAh. Landing isn't optimized: descent is prescribed at 5 m/s down to 10 ft and 1 m/s below that, drawing the 40%-throttle current scaled linearly to 30% and then 20% throttle. The payload rides the outbound 8 nm leg only.

The limit I know about: capacity is the rated mAh, flat. Peukert derating is a lead-acid effect that barely touches Li-ion at drone C-rates, but voltage sag under load can trip the cutoff before the rated capacity is drawn. For a high-current or aged pack I'd expect roughly 10–30% less usable capacity. That range is an order-of-magnitude estimate from hobbyist sources, and modeling it properly needs internal-resistance data I don't have.

The transition leg

A winged tailsitter's hover-to-cruise transition can't take that shortcut. The rotor thrust has to physically rotate from vertical to horizontal as the wing builds lift with airspeed, so the required thrust angle and the drag area both keep changing through the maneuver. I integrate that leg as a full quaternion 6DOF rigid body, position, velocity, attitude, and angular rate together. Attitude kinematics: q̇ = ½ q ⊗ [0, ω] (Hamilton, scalar-first), alongside the rotational dynamics I ω̇ = τ − ω × (Iω). I use quaternions specifically because Euler angles hit a gimbal-lock singularity right at the 90° pitch-over a tailsitter transition goes through.

Lift points along the wing's own body-fixed axis and scales with the velocity component perpendicular to it, rather than pointing straight up and scaling with raw airspeed the way a simpler model would. That distinction matters most mid-transition, when the vehicle is neither purely hovering nor purely cruising and a fixed lift direction isn't a safe approximation.

Attitude controller, lift direction, and inertia
e=log(q−1⊗qtarget)τ=clip(kpIe−kdIω,±τmax) L→=12ρCLS|v⊥|2a^v⊥=v−(v·a^)a^ Ix=m(0.25b2)2Iy=m(0.35l2)2Iz=Ix+Iy

The log map returns the shortest-path rotation vector, an angle of 2·atan2(|v|, w) wrapped to ±π. The lift axis â is the body −x axis, which points straight up at cruise attitude and edge-on at hover. Inertia comes from Roskam's radii of gyration for light aircraft (b is wingspan, l is body length), and τmax is the single-motor thrust range times a half-length arm, with yaw authority set to 20% of pitch. Those are estimates until CAD gives real principal moments, and I can override them per vehicle. I integrate with an adaptive solver (rtol 1e-6, max step 0.5 s) until forward speed reaches the target or the distance cap.

Wing Optimizer

A wing-sizing tool that searches lift coefficient, aspect ratio, and taper ratio for whichever combination minimizes structural mass, cruise drag, or total mission energy. Three of its search objectives are grounded in real aerostructural optimization papers. It doesn't claim to replicate methods like RANS CFD, nonlinear FE, or flight test — a standalone script can't actually run those.

DisciplineOptimization tool
Year2026
Built withPython
Repositoryexs783/Log_Reader

Overview

wing_optimizer.py sizes a wing from mission requirements alone. It takes how much weight the wing has to lift and how fast the aircraft cruises, then searches lift coefficient, aspect ratio, and (for the energy objective) taper ratio for whichever combination minimizes structural mass, cruise drag, or total mission energy. It emits the winning geometry as SolidWorks-ready NACA equations or point-cloud curves, plus a vehicle_configs-style JSON entry Mission Model can load directly.

Three of its objectives are each grounded in one real aerostructural optimization paper. The method below covers what carried over from each paper, and what I deliberately left out of scope.

Method

Aerodynamic-center reporting is inspired by Grimm, Price & Ahmad's flight-tested flying-wing optimization (Aerospace Systems, 2026), which treats longitudinal stability as the primary design objective. wing_mac_and_ac() reports the wing's own mean aerodynamic chord and where its aerodynamic center sits relative to the root leading edge. Both come straight from closed-form wing-theory integrals for the linear-taper and elliptical planform, not from a table: c̄ = (2/3)c_root(1+λ+λ²)/(1+λ), x_AC = ȳ·tan(Λ) + 0.25c̄. This only covers the wing's own half of a stability check. A real static margin also needs the aircraft's CG and any tail, which is out of scope for a wing-only script.

Two wing-mass models. --mass-model empirical is a single fitted AR^0.5 exponent. --mass-model beam is inspired by Zhao et al.'s finite-element model updating for a composite flying-wing (AIAA SciTech 2019, 2019-1814), which calibrates mass and stiffness against real vibration-test data. It makes two real structural drivers explicit instead: ultimate load factor and airfoil thickness ratio, through a first-order beam-bending scaling, m ∝ (AR/AR_ref)^1.5 · (n/n_ref) · (t/c_ref ÷ t/c)^0.5. The AR penalty is steeper, load factor enters linearly, and thinner airfoils cost more mass. It's a physically-derived scaling, not a named curve fit, so it isn't borrowing anyone else's exact coefficients.

Joint taper search. --objective combined searches taper jointly with cl and aspect ratio, inspired by Bombardieri et al.'s coupled aerostructural wing optimization (Structural and Multidisciplinary Optimization, 2021). Their central finding is that optimizing aerodynamics and structure separately misses real gains a coupled search finds. Taper's only channel into this model is bending relief to wing mass to hover energy, η_bend = 2(1+2λ)/(3(1+λ)), weighed against induced drag from the Oswald-efficiency estimate, e₀ = 4.61(1−0.045·AR^0.68)cos(Λ)^0.15 − 3.1, C_Di = C_L²/(π·AR·e₀). That's a narrower coupling than the paper's full aero-structural one, but it's real: a taper that trades structural weight for nothing in cruise drag gets evaluated by the same objective as cl and AR, not decided in a separate step.

Sizing the wing

Everything starts from the lift equation. The wing has to carry the lift mass at cruise speed, so the lift coefficient fixes the required area, and the aspect ratio then splits that area into span and chord.

Area, span, and chord
S=2mgρCLv2b=AR·Sc¯=Sb croot=2c¯1+λctip=λcroot(elliptical:croot=4Sπb)

Area scales as 1/v², the same relationship that makes Mission Model's cruise speed matter so much. Mean chord is S/b regardless of taper; taper only changes how that mean splits between root and tip.

The energy objective

The energy objective adds two costs that pull in opposite directions. A higher aspect ratio cuts induced drag, which saves cruise energy. It also makes the wing heavier, and a heavier wing costs more energy to hover. The search finds where those cancel.

Total mission energy for one candidate wing
E=D·v·tcruise+Phover·thover D=12ρv2S(CD0+CDi)CDi=CL2π·AR·e0 Phover=[(m+mwing)g]3/2FM·2ρAdiskAdisk=mdisk loading

The hover term is ideal momentum-theory induced power divided by a figure of merit. FM = 0.6, from a multicopter endurance paper that states typical propellers land between 0.5 and 0.7 (arXiv:2109.04741). The disk is fixed hardware sized once at the baseline mass, so a heavier wing changes the weight the disk lifts, not its area. e₀ is Raymer's fit up to AR 13, and above that I switch to Obert's e₀ = 1/(1.05 + 0.007π·AR), because Raymer's fit crosses zero near AR 18.5. The search is a 100 × 100 grid over CL and AR, with a 20-step taper search added under the combined objective.

Log Dashboard

Live demo →

A Flask + Plotly dashboard that decodes ArduCopter/PX4 flight logs into a full diagnostic report. Upload your own flight log in the live demo and get back the score, the issues list, and every chart. The numbers on this page come from a real sample log.

DisciplineFlight-data tooling
Year2026
Built withPython, Flask, Plotly, pymavlink
Repositoryexs783/Log_Reader

Overview

Every ArduPilot-based flight controller (Pixhawk and compatibles) writes a binary .BIN log of everything it measured mid-flight, hundreds of message types at up to a few hundred Hz. Reading that raw log to answer "was this flight okay, and if not, why" normally means a separate desktop tool (MAVExplorer) and manual digging. This dashboard does that automatically. Upload a log and get back attitude tracking, vibration spectra, motor balance, PID tracking, GPS track, battery health, and barometer/magnetometer data across dedicated tabs, plus one overall flight-quality verdict.

It's built for the same audience Mission Model is: someone who just flew a real airframe and needs to know whether the motors, frame, or tuning are actually behaving before flying again, not a generic telemetry viewer.

Method

The backend (python_backend.py) reads a raw .BIN log with pymavlink, walks its message stream, and classifies the airframe from its frame-type parameter (quad/hex/octo/X8/tri/Y3/Y6). Vibration comes from the IMU's three accelerometer axes, and I look at it two ways: how big it is, and what frequency it lives at.

Ten analysis passes run per log: IMU, vibration, attitude, battery, motors, PID/rate controller, GPS, barometer, magnetometer, and discrete flight events (mode changes, arming, errors), each producing its own stats block. A summary pass then scores the flight 0–100 and lists specific issues (e.g. "poor roll stability," "motor sync issues") instead of just dumping charts and leaving the reader to spot problems.

The dashboard (log_dashboard.html) renders the decoded series with Plotly, plus a Custom Plot tab that lets you overlay any two decoded variables against time regardless of category. That's useful for correlating, say, vibration against throttle.

Vibration

I estimate the sample rate from the median gap between timestamps. Size comes from combining the three axes into one magnitude and taking its RMS, which I grade against fixed thresholds. Frequency comes from Welch's method, which averages the spectra of overlapping segments and gives a smoother estimate than one raw FFT, plus a raw FFT to pick out the strongest peak in the 20–500 Hz motor band.

Magnitude, sample rate, and the dominant frequency
|a|=ax2+ay2+az2arms=⟨|a|2⟩fs=106median(Δtµs) X(f)=|ℱ{a(t)}|=|∫a(t)e−i2πftdt|f*=argmax20≤f≤500Xz(f)

arms under 30 m/s² grades good, 30–60 elevated, and above 60 critical. Each Welch segment is about two seconds of samples, never fewer than 256, and I plot the square root of the PSD so the axis reads closer to acceleration units. The Z-axis peak f* is what the dashboard suggests as a notch-filter frequency. This is the same analysis MAVExplorer does for ArduPilot logs, run automatically.

The flight-quality score

The score is a deduction ledger, not a learned model. It starts at 100 and every check that trips subtracts a fixed penalty, so you can always trace a number back to the finding that cost it.

Score and verdict
S=max(0,100−∑ipi)pi∈{5,10,15,20}

Penalties: critical vibration −20 (elevated −10); accelerometer noise above 15 m/s² −10 per axis; attitude error RMS above 5 −5 per axis; voltage sag above 2 V −10; minimum voltage below 10 V −15; motor imbalance above 15% −10; motor sync error above 5 −5; motor correlation below 0.85 −5; rate-controller error RMS above 10 −5 per axis; more than 5 flight events −10. Verdicts: 90 and up Excellent, 75 Good, 60 Fair, 40 Poor, below that Critical. The sample flight lost 35 points across 7 findings and scored 65, Fair.

Liquid Rocket

Ground-station software for CWRU's Liquid Propulsion Team, rebuilt from two prototypes that were never actually integrated into one working app, one of them my own original hot-fire GUI.

TeamCWRU Liquid Propulsion
Year2026
Built withPyQt6
Repositoryexs783/Liquid-Rocket

Overview

On CWRU's Liquid Propulsion Team (Aug 2025 – Jan 2026) I designed the mechanical thrust-control hardware for hot-fire tests, solenoids and servos regulating propellant flow, and built the PyQt5 control GUI that ran the hot-fire sequences against it.

A liquid rocket static test (or hot-fire test) needs a ground station: software running on a laptop near the test stand that shows the person running the test what's happening in real time, tank and chamber pressures, temperatures, thrust (via a load cell), and lets them open or close valves and fire the igniter without touching the hardware directly. Every reading also needs to be logged, since post-test analysis (did the chamber pressure spike, did a valve stick) depends on having the full data, not just what was visible live.

This app is that ground station, rebuilt: a P&ID (piping-and-instrumentation diagram) view for valve control, a live-gauge view for the 9 real sensor channels, and a logging view that records a full test session to CSV.

Method

The repo held two separate PyQt prototypes on incompatible Qt bindings: my own original PyQt5 data-logging page (main_v2.py) from the actual hot-fire tests, and a separate PyQt6 P&ID valve-control page. Its own readme said as much: "still needs to be integrated with the other page."

I rebuilt it as one PyQt6 app (ground_station/) sharing a single sensor/valve state across three tabs: Controls (the real P&ID diagram plus working valve toggles), Live Data (one gauge per real sensor channel), and Logging (start/stop to the documented raw + interpolated CSV pair).

The wire protocol (P0–P6, T0, L0, one name,value,timestamp line per reading) matches the real Teensy firmware exactly, so a fake data source stands in for hardware without changing anything else. That's verified with a headless smoke test that boots the real window, ticks it, toggles a valve, and checks a logging session actually writes CSVs.

The live demo linked below is a from-scratch JavaScript port of that same protocol and gauge math, not a screen recording. It runs the identical random-walk sensor simulation and the identical SVG gauge/valve drawing code the Python app uses, so what you click there is what the desktop app actually does with no hardware attached.

NACA to SolidWorks

Open the tool →

Type a NACA 4- or 5-digit airfoil and a chord length, and it downloads XYZ point files for SolidWorks' Curve Through XYZ Points. It does the whole calculation in your browser, so there's nothing to install and nothing uploaded.

DisciplineCAD tooling
Year2026
Built withJavaScript, SVG
Repositoryexs783/ethansimon.me

Overview

SolidWorks has no built-in NACA airfoil generator. You either trace one from a picture or paste coordinates in from a website you hope is right. I wanted the exact surface, at whatever chord the part needs, without leaving the browser. So the page evaluates the NACA equations directly and writes the points out as tab-separated X Y Z text, which is the format Curve Through XYZ Points reads. The curve lands in the Front plane at Z = 0.

It downloads three files: the upper surface, the lower surface, or one closed loop running tail, nose, tail. The chord you type is the scale, and the units are whatever your SolidWorks document uses.

I built it as the manual counterpart to Wing Optimizer, which writes the same kind of geometry out as equations. This one is for the moment you just need one airfoil.

Method

Thickness and surface points

Every NACA airfoil is a camber line with a thickness distribution wrapped around it, perpendicular to the camber line. For thickness fraction t (the last two digits, over 100), the thickness at chordwise station x is a fixed polynomial. The surface points shift each station up and down by yt along the camber line's local normal.

Thickness, then upper and lower surface
yt=5t(0.2969x−0.1260x−0.3516x2+0.2843x3−0.1036x4) θ=arctandycdx xu,l=x∓ytsinθ yu,l=yc±ytcosθ

The published polynomial ends in −0.1015x⁴, which leaves the tail slightly open. At the trailing edge the bracket is 0.2969 − 0.1260 − 0.3516 + 0.2843 − 0.1015 = 0.0021, so a 12% airfoil is 0.00126 of a chord thick there. CAD wants one sharp tail point, so I use −0.1036. The bracket then sums to zero and the surfaces meet exactly. Points are cosine spaced, x = ½(1 − cos(πi/79)), 80 per surface, which puts them densest at the nose where the curvature is.

The camber line

For 4-digit airfoils, the first digit is maximum camber m (over 100) and the second is its position p (over 10). The camber line is two parabolas that meet at x = p with matching slope.

4-digit camber line
yc=mp2(2px−x2)for x < p yc=m(1−p)2((1−2p)+2px−x2)for x ≥ p

The 5-digit series is different. The first digit fixes the design lift coefficient at 0.15 times the digit, so 2xxxx is 0.3. The next two digits give the position of maximum camber and whether the camber line is standard (2p0) or reflex (2p1). The camber line is a cubic whose constants r, k₁, and k₂/k₁ come from NACA's published table, and one expression covers both variants by setting k₂/k₁ to zero for the standard line.

I didn't want to type that table in and trust it. Thin-airfoil theory gives the ideal lift coefficient of any camber line as Cl,i = 2∫ (dyc/dx) cos θ dθ with x = ½(1 − cos θ), so every 2xx line in the table should come out at 0.300. I integrated each one numerically. 230, 240, and 250 give 0.300, and 220 gives 0.302. 210 gives 0.308 and the reflex lines run 0.301 to 0.305, which is the rounding in NACA's published constants and not a transcription error. Together the constants sit within 3% of the target.

6-series and other families need a numerical solve for their camber lines. The tool says it doesn't support them instead of guessing.

Hexacopter Drone

Design, manufacturing, and testing of a hexacopter drone for the VFS DBVF competition. I contributed to the frame, motor mounts, sensor mounts, and payload mounts.

DisciplineCAD
Built withSolidWorks
CompetitionVFS DBVF
AssemblyCompDrone2025

Overview

CompDrone2025 is the full hexacopter assembly built for the VFS Design-Build-Vertical-Flight competition: carbon-fiber tube arms, a machined frame, RTK GPS and Pixhawk avionics mounts, a servo-actuated payload-drop mechanism, and folding landing gear, all as real mated SolidWorks assemblies. The model below is the actual assembly, exported to glTF so it's something you can rotate rather than a fixed render. It is also a platform: a number of different payloads can be attached to it, which is why the payload mount is a subassembly of its own.

Method

The assembly is organized the way a real drone build actually breaks down: ARM (carbon-fiber tube, motor mount plate/ring, prop), FRM (top/bottom plates, payload mount, protective dome), LGR (landing-gear struts, TPU feet), BATT (battery + velcro strap + printed mount), CAM, PAYLOAD (servo, horn, clamp body, water-bottle attachment), and ELEC (Pixhawk 6X, RTK GPS, baseboard) — seven subassemblies mated into one top-level .SLDASM, each with real vendor parts (T-Motor, Amass, Pixhawk) modeled or imported alongside the machined/printed parts.

Flexural Fatigue Test Machine

A flexural fatigue test machine for internal testing, built to ASTM F1387 A6. I designed, manufactured, and implemented it as a Mechanical Engineering Co-Op at Tylok International.

DisciplineMechanical testing
EmployerTylok International
DatesJan – Aug 2026
StandardASTM F1387 A6
Test loadOver 2,000 lb
Cycle rangeOver 80,000

Overview

The machine is for internal testing, built in accordance with ASTM standards to handle a variety of parts and sizes. I owned it end to end: the design, the machining and assembly, and the verification. The test load is over 2,000 lb and the cycle range is over 80,000. The photo shows the assembled machine, with the ASTM F1387 A6 flexure-fatigue label on its controller.

I also developed other internal stress and verification apparatus: thermal, flow, tensile, and pressure testing.

Method

Stress analysis first. I used fundamental principles of stress analysis to decide how to design the system and what its size constraints were, before any CAD.

CAD. With the approach and the size limits set, I moved into CAD.

Machining and assembly. I machined the parts and assembled the machine.

Verification. I verified the machine with strain gauge readings and the data I collected, and confirmed the results against them.

Data collection and control. I researched, acquired, and integrated a Dewesoft system for data acquisition. I wrote Dewesoft programs (DewesoftX and C++) to collect and analyse the data and to run the machine's control system.

X-8 Tailsitter

An adaptation of the Hexacopter Drone into an X-8 tailsitter frame, focused on generating lift from a wing and a high thrust-to-weight ratio.

DisciplineCAD
Built withSolidWorks
Derived fromHexacopter Drone

Overview

Designed as a derivative of the hexacopter platform. Rather than six rotors on a flat frame, the X-8 layout pairs eight motors (coaxial or square-8 arrangement) beneath a wing so the airframe can pitch over into wing-borne cruise. The design goal is a high thrust-to-weight ratio so the vehicle can still transition and recover reliably even before the wing is generating full lift.

Tail Sitter Designs

An ongoing project: I'm teaching newer VTOL members how to design their own tail sitters, so the club can design successful transition drones.

DisciplineCAD, teaching
Built withSolidWorks
StatusOngoing

Overview

Tail-sitters are the simplest wing-based VTOL transition mechanism. The whole airframe pitches over rather than tilting individual motors or wing sections, which makes them the right starting point for members who haven't designed a transitioning aircraft before. The current design under review is a biplane tail-sitter: a small quadcopter core with foils that double as landing gear, weighing an S1223 airfoil against NACA 4412/6412 candidates.

Design decisions worked through with the team include 2 vs. 4 rotors, one wing vs. two, and how the wings attach to the frame (a matching profile clamp, bolted to the frame). Transition logic gets validated in PX4 + Gazebo SITL simulation before any hardware flies.

Outcome

Transition mechanism
CAD not yet finalized — diagram of the mechanism being designed around: the whole airframe pitches from hover to cruise as one rigid body, no separate tilt actuator.

Tilt Rotor Designs

Similar to Tail Sitter Designs. I teach newer VTOL members how to design tilt rotors and build their own within a semester.

DisciplineCAD, teaching
Built withSolidWorks
StatusCAD not yet finalized

Overview

Where a tail-sitter pitches the whole airframe, a tilt-rotor only rotates the motors from vertical to near-horizontal, letting the fuselage stay level through the transition. That's more mechanically complex than a tail-sitter, but easier on payloads and sensors that need to stay upright. It's the natural next step once members have a tail-sitter under their belt.

Outcome

Transition mechanism
CAD not yet finalized — diagram of the mechanism being designed around: only the rotor nacelles rotate, the fuselage stays level so payloads and sensors don't have to.

DARPA Lift 2028

A drone built to compete in the DARPA LIFT '28 challenge. The current design is a tail sitter with a low rotor count, being actively adapted.

DisciplineCAD
Built withSolidWorks
CompetitionDARPA LIFT '28
StatusIn progress

Overview

The CAD counterpart to Mission Model, which sweeps motor/prop/battery combinations against a mock DARPA Heavy Lift 2028 mission. This is the physical airframe that mission analysis is meant to inform. Current direction is a low-rotor-count tail sitter, prioritizing hover efficiency and cruise range for the heavy-lift mission profile over the redundancy a higher rotor count would buy.

Outcome

Rotor-count trade-off
CAD not yet finalized — the actual design trade-off driving the current direction: 2 rotors over 6, trading redundancy for hover efficiency and cruise range on the heavy-lift mission.
